### glaciers | long | Earth science
Glaciers are often described as rivers of ice, but the comparison conceals a crucial difference in how they move. A river flows because water deforms freely under its own weight; ice, by contrast, is a crystalline solid that deforms only slowly, creeping at rates of a few meters per year. Yet many glaciers advance hundreds of meters annually. The explanation lies not within the ice but beneath it.

Where meltwater collects at the base of a glacier, it can reduce friction between ice and bedrock so dramatically that the glacier slides as a single mass. In some cases the water is pressurized enough to lift the ice slightly, producing sudden surges in which a glacier moves many times faster than normal for months at a time. This basal sliding helps explain a puzzle that long troubled glaciologists: why two glaciers of similar size and slope can move at wildly different speeds. The answer depends less on the ice itself than on the plumbing hidden underneath it, which is extraordinarily difficult to observe directly.

### patronage | long | Art history
Renaissance painters are often imagined as solitary geniuses, yet most worked under contracts that specified their subjects, dimensions, and even the quantity of expensive pigments they were to use. A patron commissioning an altarpiece in fifteenth-century Florence might require that the Virgin's robe be painted in ultramarine of a particular grade, a blue made from ground lapis lazuli that cost more than gold. Such clauses were not mere fussiness: they allowed the patron to display wealth and piety in a form that viewers of the time could read at a glance.

Over the following century, however, contracts increasingly emphasized the painter's own hand rather than the materials. Patrons began to specify that the master himself, not his assistants, must paint the principal figures. Historians have read this shift as evidence of a changing idea of value: skill, rather than costly substance, was becoming what buyers believed they were paying for. The change did not happen everywhere at once, and some patrons continued to insist on precious materials well into the sixteenth century, but its general direction is clear.

### antibiotics | short | Medicine
Bacteria can become resistant to antibiotics through random mutation, but a faster route is borrowing. Many bacteria swap small loops of DNA, called plasmids, with neighboring cells, even cells of different species. A single plasmid can carry genes for resistance to several drugs at once, so exposure to one antibiotic can select for bacteria that happen to resist others as well. This helps explain why resistance sometimes appears to drugs that a bacterial population has never encountered.

### cities-language | long | Linguistics
Languages spoken in large, well-connected societies tend to have simpler grammar than those spoken by small, isolated communities. At first this seems backward: one might expect the languages of complex civilizations to be the most elaborate. Some linguists propose an explanation rooted in who learns the language. Where many adults learn a language as newcomers—through trade, migration, or conquest—features that adult learners find hard, such as irregular verb forms and intricate case endings, tend to erode over generations.

In small communities, by contrast, nearly everyone acquires the language in childhood, and children master irregularities with ease. Such languages can therefore retain, and even accumulate, complexity. The hypothesis is attractive but difficult to test, because the factors involved—population size, contact with outsiders, and the proportion of adult learners—tend to rise and fall together, making it hard to tell which one is actually responsible.

### argument-12 | argument | Critical reasoning

### argument-13 | argument | Critical reasoning
Since the company introduced a four-day work week, employee productivity per hour has increased by 20 percent. Therefore, the company's total output must have increased.

?? mc | The argument depends on which of the following assumptions?

* The increase in productivity per hour was large enough to make up for the reduction in hours worked.
- Other companies will adopt the four-day week.

### migration-birds | long | Animal behavior
Migratory songbirds that fly at night must navigate without landmarks, and researchers have long debated which cues they rely on. Experiments in planetariums in the 1960s showed that young birds learn to orient by the rotation of the night sky, identifying the point around which the stars turn. Later work demonstrated that birds can also sense Earth's magnetic field, and that they calibrate this magnetic compass against the setting sun each evening.

These findings raised a new question: when the cues disagree, which one wins? In several species, the answer appears to change with age. Inexperienced birds give priority to celestial cues, while experienced adults rely more heavily on the magnetic field, perhaps because a lifetime of journeys has taught them which signals prove reliable. If so, navigation is not a fixed instinct but a skill refined through experience.

### coffee-trade | short | Economics
When the price of coffee rises, farmers are often urged to plant more trees. Because coffee trees take three to four years to bear fruit, however, the new supply arrives long after the price spike that prompted it, frequently at a moment when prices have already fallen. The added harvest then pushes prices lower still. This lag between decision and result helps explain why coffee prices swing so dramatically from boom to bust.

### memoir | long | Literature
Readers of memoir often assume that its value lies in accuracy, and they feel betrayed when a memoirist is shown to have invented or rearranged events. Yet the genre has always involved some shaping. Memory is not a recording, and any account of a life must select, compress, and order experiences to make them intelligible. A memoir that recorded every conversation would be both impossible to write and unreadable.

The real question, then, is not whether a memoirist shapes the past but where legitimate shaping ends and fabrication begins. Most readers and writers draw the line at invention: compressing three conversations into one may be acceptable, but reporting a conversation that never happened is not. Even this line blurs in practice, since a remembered conversation is itself partly a reconstruction. Still, the distinction matters, because a reader's trust depends on the belief that the events described, however arranged, actually occurred.

### fermentation | long | History of science
In the 1850s, most chemists believed that fermentation was a purely chemical process: sugar broke down into alcohol through the action of decaying matter, with no need for living organisms. Yeast cells, visible under the microscope, were regarded as a by-product rather than a cause. Louis Pasteur challenged this view by showing that fermentation occurred only in the presence of living yeast and stopped when the yeast was killed.

Pasteur's conclusion that fermentation required life was itself later revised. In 1897 Eduard Buchner ground yeast cells, filtered out every living cell, and found that the remaining juice could still ferment sugar. The active agents were enzymes—chemical substances produced by the yeast. In a sense, both sides of the earlier debate had been partly right: fermentation depends on substances made by living cells, but once made, those substances can do their work without the cells themselves.
